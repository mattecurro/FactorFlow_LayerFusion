\subsection{Eyeriss: Weight Register Sizing (CS1)}
\label{sec:eyeriss-cs1}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

Every PE in the Eyeriss-Like architecture contains a local weight
register (WReg) that holds the weight residuals of all fused layers,
i.e.\ the channel and filter-size factors ($C \times R$ per layer)
that the spatial array rows cannot absorb.
Under full fusion the cumulative footprint grows with the number of
layers and the width of their channels: for ResNet18 (17 fused layers)
it ranges from roughly 460 to over 14\,000 entries depending on
the PE configuration, making WReg the single tightest feasibility
constraint.

The core design question is therefore:
\emph{given a PE budget, what is the minimum WReg that admits at least
one feasible fused mapping?}
A larger register relaxes the constraint and enables smaller, cheaper
PE arrays, but every weight access then touches a larger SRAM
structure, inflating energy.
CS1 quantifies this trade-off by sweeping a grid of PE configurations
($\text{rows} \times \text{cols}$, up to $\sim$17\,000 total PEs) at
several WReg sizes.
For each PE budget the experiment identifies the smallest WReg that
yields at least one feasible mapping.
The GlobalBuffer (128\,KB), input register, intermediate register, and
output register are held constant throughout.

\subsubsection{Results}

\paragraph{Weight-dominant workloads (VGG16, ResNet18).}
Table~\ref{tab:eyeriss-cs1-wreg} reports the minimum WReg size
required at three PE budgets, together with the configuration chosen
by the mapper and the resulting energy, latency, and EDP.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering

    For each row the PE budget is fixed and the smallest feasible WReg
    is reported.}
  \label{tab:eyeriss-cs1-wreg}
  \begin{tabular}{llrrrrr}
    \toprule
    Workload & PE budget & Min WReg & Config & Energy ($\mu$J) &
      Latency (cc) & EDP (J$\cdot$cc) \\
    \midrule
    VGG16    & 16\,384 & 1\,200  & $256 \times 64$  & $3.47 \times 10^{5}$
             & $5.46 \times 10^{6}$ & $1.94 \times 10^{6}$ \\
    VGG16    & 8\,192  & 2\,400  & $128 \times 64$  & $5.42 \times 10^{5}$
             & $5.68 \times 10^{6}$ & $3.12 \times 10^{6}$ \\
    VGG16    & 4\,096  & 4\,800  & $128 \times 32$  & $9.33 \times 10^{5}$
             & $6.72 \times 10^{6}$ & $6.29 \times 10^{6}$ \\
    \midrule
    ResNet18 & 16\,384 & 902     & $256 \times 64$  & $4.71 \times 10^{4}$
             & $3.04 \times 10^{6}$ & $1.48 \times 10^{5}$ \\
    ResNet18 & 8\,192  & 1\,800  & $128 \times 64$  & $6.85 \times 10^{4}$
             & $3.04 \times 10^{6}$ & $2.13 \times 10^{5}$ \\
    ResNet18 & 4\,096  & 3\,600  & $128 \times 32$  & $1.12 \times 10^{5}$
             & $3.06 \times 10^{6}$ & $3.46 \times 10^{5}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The table reveals a remarkably regular scaling law.
For ResNet18 a designer with a 16\,384-PE budget needs at least
WReg\,=\,902 entries, and only two PE configurations out of the entire
grid are feasible at that size.
Halving the PE budget to 8\,192 doubles the required register to
WReg\,=\,1\,800, and halving again to 4\,096 doubles it once more to
WReg\,=\,3\,600.
The mechanism is straightforward: fewer spatial rows leave more weight
factors unabsorbed, and those residuals must be stored temporally in
the register.
VGG16 obeys the same doubling rule (1\,200 $\to$ 2\,400 $\to$ 4\,800)
at proportionally larger WReg values, reflecting its deeper network
(13 fused layers) and wider channels.

The energy consequence is substantial.
For ResNet18, moving from the 16\,384-PE point
($E = 4.71 \times 10^{4}$\,$\mu$J) to the 4\,096-PE point
($E = 1.12 \times 10^{5}$\,$\mu$J) increases energy by $2.4\times$,
because each weight access now touches a register that is $4\times$
larger.
Latency, however, stays nearly constant
($\approx 3.04$--$3.06 \times 10^{6}$\,cc), which indicates that the
computation is not memory-bound once a feasible mapping exists.
The EDP therefore tracks energy closely, growing by $2.3\times$ over
the same range.
VGG16 shows both a steeper energy increase ($2.7\times$) and a
moderate latency increase ($5.46 \to 6.72 \times 10^{6}$\,cc), so its
EDP rises by $3.2\times$ from the largest to the smallest PE budget.

Figure~\ref{fig:eyeriss-cs1} visualises this trade-off through two
summary curves.
Summary~A selects, for each WReg, the feasible configuration with the
fewest PEs, representing the cheapest array that works.
Summary~B selects the configuration with the lowest EDP, which may use
a larger array.
The two curves coincide for ResNet18 at WReg\,=\,902 and
WReg\,=\,1\,800, where the minimum-PE configuration is also the
minimum-EDP one.
They diverge for VGG16 at WReg\,=\,2\,400, where Summary~B prefers
16\,384~PEs ($128 \times 128$, EDP\,=\,$3.10 \times 10^{6}$) over the
8\,192-PE minimum that Summary~A picks
(EDP\,=\,$3.12 \times 10^{6}$): the latency reduction from a larger
array more than offsets its higher area cost.

\paragraph{Activation-dominant workloads (FSRCNN, MC-CNN).}
For these workloads the weight register is not the binding constraint.
FSRCNN reaches feasibility at WReg\,=\,200 with only 512~PEs
($128 \times 4$), and raising WReg to 300 or 400 does not reduce the
required PE count; all three sizes yield 12 feasible configurations.
MC-CNN requires WReg\,$\ge$\,200 for 1\,024~PEs ($64 \times 16$);
only at the smallest tested value (WReg\,=\,100) does the minimum
rise to 2\,048~PEs.
Energy still scales linearly with register size, but the effect is
modest because the register is small in absolute terms.

Figure~\ref{fig:eyeriss-cs1-feas} summarises the feasibility picture
for all four workloads: the bars show how many PE configurations are
feasible at each WReg size, while the line tracks the minimum PE
count.
The contrast is striking: VGG16 and ResNet18 gain feasibility steeply
as WReg grows (from 2 to 8 configurations), whereas FSRCNN and MC-CNN
are already saturated at the smallest tested sizes.

\paragraph{Key takeaway.}
The weight register is the critical sizing knob for the Eyeriss-Like
architecture under full fusion.
For weight-dominant workloads, halving the PE budget roughly doubles
the required WReg, and the larger register inflates energy in
proportion, because every weight read touches a physically larger SRAM
structure.
Latency is largely unaffected, so EDP tracks energy closely.
For activation-dominant workloads the register is non-binding and can
be kept small without limiting feasibility.
