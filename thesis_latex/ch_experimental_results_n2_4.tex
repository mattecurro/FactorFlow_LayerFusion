\subsection{Eyeriss: Weight Register Sizing (CS1)}
\label{sec:eyeriss-cs1}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

In the Eyeriss-Like architecture every PE contains a local weight
register (WReg) that stores the weight residuals of all fused layers,
i.e.\ the channel and filter-size factors ($C \times R$ per layer) not
absorbed by the spatial array rows.
When many layers are fused, the cumulative WReg requirement can become
the single tightest constraint on feasibility: for ResNet18 (17
fused layers) the required WReg ranges from roughly 460 to over
14\,000 entries depending on the PE configuration.

A designer choosing a PE count for a given chip area budget needs to
know: \emph{what is the minimum weight register size that makes the
fused mapping feasible at that PE count?}
Conversely, relaxing the WReg, i.e.\ allocating more on-chip SRAM per
PE, opens up smaller PE arrays that may be cheaper to manufacture but
come at an energy cost, because a larger register is accessed at every
weight read.

CS1 answers this question by evaluating a full grid of PE
configurations ($\text{rows} \times \text{cols}$, up to
$\sim$17\,000 total PEs) at several WReg sizes.
For each PE budget the experiment identifies the smallest WReg that
yields at least one feasible mapping.
The GlobalBuffer (128\,KB), input register, intermediate register, and
output register are held constant.

\subsubsection{Results}

\paragraph{Weight-dominant workloads (VGG16, ResNet18).}
These workloads stress the weight register most heavily.
Table~\ref{tab:eyeriss-cs1-wreg} presents the results read in the
designer-oriented direction: for a given PE budget, the table shows the
minimum WReg size required and the resulting energy and EDP.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Eyeriss CS1 — Minimum WReg size for a given PE budget
    (VGG16 and ResNet18, full fusion).}
  \label{tab:eyeriss-cs1-wreg}
  \begin{tabular}{llrrrrr}
    \toprule
    Workload & PE budget & Min WReg & Config & Energy ($\mu$J) &
      Latency (cc) & EDP (J$\cdot$cc) \\
    \midrule
    VGG16    & 16\,384 & 1\,200  & $256 \times 64$  & $3.47 \times 10^{5}$
             & $5.46 \times 10^{6}$ & $1.94 \times 10^{6}$ \\
    VGG16    & 8\,192  & 2\,400  & $128 \times 64$  & $5.42 \times 10^{5}$
             & $5.68 \times 10^{6}$ & $3.12 \times 10^{6}$ \\
    VGG16    & 4\,096  & 4\,800  & $128 \times 32$  & $9.33 \times 10^{5}$
             & $6.72 \times 10^{6}$ & $6.29 \times 10^{6}$ \\
    \midrule
    ResNet18 & 16\,384 & 902    & $256 \times 64$  & $4.71 \times 10^{4}$
             & $3.04 \times 10^{6}$ & $1.48 \times 10^{5}$ \\
    ResNet18 & 8\,192  & 1\,800  & $128 \times 64$  & $6.85 \times 10^{4}$
             & $3.04 \times 10^{6}$ & $2.13 \times 10^{5}$ \\
    ResNet18 & 4\,096  & 3\,600  & $128 \times 32$  & $1.14 \times 10^{5}$
             & $3.04 \times 10^{6}$ & $3.53 \times 10^{5}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Taking ResNet18 as an example, a designer with a budget of 16\,384~PEs
needs at least WReg\,=\,902 entries (the tightest feasible point, with
only 2 valid configurations out of the full grid).
Halving the PE budget to 8\,192 requires doubling the register to
WReg\,=\,1\,800, and halving again to 4\,096 demands
WReg\,=\,3\,600.
The trade-off is clear: each halving of the PE array roughly doubles
the minimum WReg, because fewer spatial rows force more weight factors
to be stored temporally in the register.

The energy consequence is significant.
For ResNet18, going from 16\,384~PEs (WReg\,=\,902) to 4\,096~PEs
(WReg\,=\,3\,600) increases energy by $2.4\times$
($4.71 \times 10^{4} \to 1.14 \times 10^{5}$\,$\mu$J), because every
weight access now touches a register that is $4\times$ larger.
Latency, however, remains nearly constant
($\approx 3.04 \times 10^{6}$\,cc), indicating that the bottleneck is
compute-bound rather than memory-bound once a feasible mapping exists.
The EDP therefore grows in proportion to energy, reaching $2.4\times$
the optimum at 4\,096~PEs.

VGG16 follows the same pattern but with larger absolute WReg values
(1\,200 to 4\,800), reflecting its deeper network (13 fused layers)
and wider channels.
Its energy increases by $2.7\times$ and EDP by $3.2\times$ from the
largest to the smallest PE budget.

Figure~\ref{fig:eyeriss-cs1} plots the Summary~A and Summary~B curves
for these two workloads.
Summary~B selects the minimum-EDP configuration, which may use more

\paragraph{Activation-dominant workloads (FSRCNN, MC-CNN).}
For these workloads the weight register is not the binding constraint.
FSRCNN achieves feasibility at WReg\,=\,200 with only 1\,024~PEs, and
increasing WReg to 400 does not reduce the required PE count.
MC-CNN requires WReg\,$\ge$\,200 for 1\,024~PEs; only at the
smallest tested value (WReg\,=\,100) does it need 2\,048~PEs.
Energy still scales linearly with WReg, but the effect is modest
because the register is small in absolute terms.

Figure~\ref{fig:eyeriss-cs1-feas} shows the feasibility trade-off for
all four workloads: the number of valid configurations (bars) and the
minimum PE count (line) as a function of WReg size.
The contrast between weight-dominant and activation-dominant workloads
is striking: VGG16 and ResNet18 gain feasibility steeply with larger
WReg, while FSRCNN and MC-CNN are already saturated at the smallest
tested sizes.

\paragraph{Key takeaway.}
The weight register is the critical sizing knob for the Eyeriss-Like
architecture under full fusion, particularly for weight-dominant
workloads.
Halving the PE budget roughly doubles the required WReg, and the
larger register inflates energy proportionally.
For activation-dominant workloads the register is non-binding and
can be set to a small value without affecting feasibility.
