\subsection{Eyeriss: PE Array Aspect Ratio (CS5)}
\label{sec:eyeriss-cs5}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

The previous case studies (CS1--CS3) varied the register sizes while
allowing the PE array dimensions to change freely.
CS5 asks a complementary question:
\emph{given a fixed total PE budget, how should the array be shaped,
i.e.\ how many rows versus columns, to minimise EDP?}

The aspect ratio matters because the Eyeriss spatial array maps
different loop dimensions to each axis.
Rows absorb the weight-related factors ($C$, $S$, $Z$), while columns
absorb the output-channel factor ($M$ in single-layer mode, $Z$ in
multi-layer fusion).
A row-heavy array (many rows, few columns) can absorb more weight
factors spatially but leaves larger channel residuals in the column
direction.
A column-heavy array does the opposite.
The optimal shape therefore depends on the relative magnitudes of the
weight and channel dimensions of the fused workload.

CS5 sweeps all power-of-two factorisations of a fixed PE budget
(2\,048 for FSRCNN and MC-CNN, 16\,384 for VGG16 and ResNet18) and
records energy, latency, and EDP for each feasible configuration.

\subsubsection{Results}

\paragraph{ResNet18 (16\,384 PEs).}
Table~\ref{tab:eyeriss-cs5} lists the feasible configurations for
ResNet18.
Seven aspect ratios are feasible, from the column-heavy
$256 \times 64$ to the extreme row-heavy $16{,}384 \times 1$.
Latency is constant across all configurations
($3.042 \times 10^{6}$\,cc), so the EDP ranking is determined entirely
by energy.
The best configuration is $256 \times 64$
($E = 4.709 \times 10^{4}$\,$\mu$J), and energy increases gently as
the array becomes more row-heavy, reaching
$4.816 \times 10^{4}$\,$\mu$J at $2{,}048 \times 8$ and beyond.
The total variation is only $2.3\%$, indicating that for ResNet18 the
aspect ratio has a negligible impact once feasibility is achieved.

The reason is that ResNet18's large channel dimensions ($Z$ up to 512)
can saturate even a modest number of columns: at 64~columns the SACols
mechanism already absorbs the dominant $Z$ factors, and adding more
columns yields no further benefit.
Below 64~columns the mapper must fold more $Z$ residuals into the
weight register, increasing its access cost slightly.

\paragraph{VGG16 (16\,384 PEs).}
Only three configurations are feasible ($256 \times 64$,
$512 \times 32$, $1024 \times 16$), all with constant latency
($5.455 \times 10^{6}$\,cc).
The best EDP is $512 \times 32$
($1.93 \times 10^{6}$), closely followed by $256 \times 64$
($1.94 \times 10^{6}$).
The narrow feasibility window reflects VGG16's large WReg requirement
(CS1): extreme row-heavy configs cannot fit the weight residuals
within the configured register.

\paragraph{FSRCNN (2\,048 PEs).}
FSRCNN exhibits a broader feasibility range, with all nine
power-of-two factorisations from $8 \times 256$ to $2{,}048 \times 1$
being feasible.
The best EDP is the most column-heavy configuration,
$8 \times 256$ ($E = 1.184 \times 10^{5}$\,$\mu$J,
EDP\,=\,$2.39 \times 10^{6}$).
Energy is $5.5\%$ lower at $8 \times 256$ than at $64 \times 32$ and
beyond, where it plateaus at $1.253 \times 10^{5}$\,$\mu$J.
The column-heavy preference arises because FSRCNN's tiny channel
counts ($Z \in \{1, 12, 56\}$) benefit from wide column arrays that
can distribute the $Z$ factors across more SACols groups, reducing
the per-PE intermediate-activation footprint.

\paragraph{MC-CNN (2\,048 PEs).}
All nine configurations are feasible.
The best EDP is $16 \times 128$
($E = 1.596 \times 10^{5}$\,$\mu$J,
EDP\,=\,$2.09 \times 10^{6}$), tying with $128 \times 16$ through
$2{,}048 \times 1$ on EDP but offering the lowest latency
($1.223 \times 10^{7}$\,cc).
The most column-heavy config ($8 \times 256$) has higher latency
($2.344 \times 10^{7}$\,cc) because only 8 rows cannot absorb enough
weight factors spatially, forcing more temporal iterations.
MC-CNN therefore favours a moderate aspect ratio that balances row
absorption of weights with column distribution of channels.

\paragraph{Cross-workload comparison.}
Figure~\ref{fig:eyeriss-cs5} presents the energy (top row) and EDP
(bottom row) for all four workloads.
Two patterns emerge.
First, the weight-dominant workloads (VGG16, ResNet18) show nearly
flat curves with very few feasible points, because the large WReg
requirement eliminates extreme aspect ratios.
Second, the activation-dominant workloads (FSRCNN, MC-CNN) display
wider feasibility and a mild preference for column-heavy
configurations, where the SACols mechanism can distribute channel
factors more effectively.
In all cases the EDP variation across feasible aspect ratios is
modest ($< 6\%$ for FSRCNN, $< 2.3\%$ for ResNet18), suggesting
that the aspect ratio is a secondary design knob compared to register
sizing (CS1--CS3).

\paragraph{Key takeaway.}
Once the register sizes are set to satisfy the fused mapping
constraints, the PE aspect ratio has limited influence on energy and
EDP.
Column-heavy configurations offer a small advantage for workloads with
narrow channels, while weight-dominant workloads are constrained to a
narrow band of balanced aspect ratios by the WReg feasibility
boundary.
The designer can therefore choose the aspect ratio primarily based
on physical layout and interconnect considerations, with confidence
that the energy impact is within a few percent of the optimum.
