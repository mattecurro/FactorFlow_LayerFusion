\subsection{Eyeriss: PE Array Aspect Ratio (CS5)}
\label{sec:eyeriss-cs5}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

CS5 address a complementary question with respect to the precedent case studies:
\emph{given a fixed total PE budget, how should the array be shaped,
i.e.\ how many rows versus columns, to minimise EDP?}

The aspect ratio matters because the Eyeriss spatial array maps
different loop dimensions to each axis.
Rows absorb the weight-related factors ($C$, $S$, $Z$), while columns
absorb the output-channel factor.

A row-heavy array (many rows, few columns) can absorb more weight
factors spatially but leaves larger channel residuals in the column
direction.
A column-heavy array does the opposite.
The optimal shape therefore depends on the relative magnitudes of the
weight and channel dimensions of the fused workload.

CS5 sweeps all power-of-two factorizations of a fixed PE budget
(2\,048 for FSRCNN and MC-CNN, 16\,384 for VGG16 and ResNet18) and
records energy, latency, and EDP for each feasible configuration.

\subsubsection{Results}

\paragraph{ResNet18 (16\,384 PEs).}
Of the fifteen power-of-two aspect ratios tested, eight are
infeasible and seven are feasible (Table~\ref{tab:eyeriss-cs5}).

\textit{Infeasibility analysis.}\enspace
The eight configurations with the most columns
($1 \times 16{,}384$ through $128 \times 128$) are infeasible because
too few rows prevent the SARows levels from absorbing enough weight
and input-channel factors spatially, leaving large residuals that
overflow the per-PE input and weight registers.
With only 1~row, no spatial factor can be absorbed at all: the weight
register requires a footprint of 10\,197~entries whereas only 902 are
available, and the input register requires 10\,216 vs.\ 400.
As the row count increases, register pressure drops progressively.
Feasibility is first achieved at $256 \times 64$, where 256~rows
provide enough spatial capacity to absorb the full $S$ and $C$
factors of every layer within the register budget.

\textit{Energy and latency analysis.}\enspace
Latency is identical across all seven feasible configurations because the DRAM transfer volume is
constant: 44.1\,M reads and 0.1\,M writes, regardless of array shape.
Energy varies by only 2.6\,\%, with $256 \times 64$ achieving the
lowest value.
The variation arises from two register levels that shift in opposite
directions as the row count increases:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{InRegister}: reads decrease by 19.2\,\%, because additional rows allow
    the SARows levels to absorb larger $S$ and $C$ factors
    spatially, reducing temporal iterations over input data.
    Writes decrease by 23.6\,\% for the same reason.
  \item \textbf{IntermediateRegister}: reads increase by 16.4\,\%
    and writes by 11.9\,\%, because fewer columns force the
    mapper to iterate over more $Z$ (intermediate output or output-channel) residuals
    temporally, increasing intermediate-activation traffic.
\end{itemize}
Weight-register reads, Global-Buffer traffic, and DRAM
traffic are constant across all seven configurations.
The IntermediateRegister increase slightly outweighs the InRegister
savings, producing the gentle 2.6\,\% upward energy trend as the
array becomes more row-heavy.
The best configuration, $256 \times 64$, maximizes the number of
columns among feasible aspect ratios, allowing SACols to absorb a
larger share of the $Z$ factors and minimise intermediate register
pressure.
Beyond $2{,}048 \times 8$, energy and EDP plateau because SARows
has already absorbed all available weight factors; additional rows
offer no further spatial benefit.

\paragraph{VGG16 (16\,384 PEs).}
Seven power-of-two factorisations from $256 \times 64$ to
$16{,}384 \times 1$ are feasible, all sharing the same latency.
The best EDP is $512 \times 32$, tied with $256 \times 64$.
Energy rises by $4.1\%$ at
$2{,}048 \times 8$ and plateaus thereafter, mirroring the ResNet18
behavior.

\paragraph{FSRCNN (2\,048 PEs).}
FSRCNN exhibits a broader feasibility range, with nine
power-of-two factorizations from $8 \times 256$ to $2{,}048 \times 1$
being feasible.
The best EDP is the most column-heavy configuration,
$8 \times 256$.
Energy is $5.7\%$ lower at $8 \times 256$ than at $64 \times 32$ and
beyond, where it plateaus.


\paragraph{MC-CNN (2\,048 PEs).}
Nine configurations are feasible from $8 \times 256$ to $2{,}048 \times 1$.
The best EDP is $128 \times 16$, tying with $256 \times 8$ through
$2{,}048 \times 1$, while $16 \times 128$ is within $0.2\%$
and offers the lowest latency among competitive configurations.
The most column-heavy config ($8 \times 256$) has higher latency because only 8 rows cannot absorb enough
weight factors spatially, forcing more temporal iterations.
MC-CNN therefore favors a moderate aspect ratio that balances row
absorption of weights with column distribution of channels.


\paragraph{Cross-workload comparison.}
Figure~\ref{fig:eyeriss-cs5} presents the energy (top row) and EDP
(bottom row) for all four workloads.
Two patterns emerge.
First, the weight-dominant workloads (VGG16, ResNet18) show nearly
flat energy and EDP curves across all seven feasible aspect ratios,
with total variation below $4.1\%$ for VGG16 and $2.6\%$ for
ResNet18.
Second, the activation-dominant workloads (FSRCNN, MC-CNN) display
a mild preference for column-heavy configurations, where the SACols
mechanism can distribute channel factors more effectively.
In all cases the EDP variation across feasible aspect ratios is
modest ($< 6\%$ for FSRCNN, $< 4.1\%$ for VGG16).

\paragraph{Considerations}
Once the register sizes are set to satisfy the fused mapping
constraints, the PE aspect ratio has limited influence on energy and
EDP.
Column-heavy configurations offer a small advantage for workloads with
narrow channels, while weight-dominant workloads show nearly flat
curves regardless of aspect ratio once registers are adequately sized.
The designer can therefore choose the aspect ratio primarily based
on physical layout and interconnect considerations, with confidence
that the energy impact is within a few percent of the optimum.
