\subsection{Full-Sized Architecture: Fusion Mode Comparison (Scenario~A)}
\label{sec:fusion-full-sized}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

The case studies presented in Sections~\ref{sec:eyeriss-cs1}
through~\ref{sec:eyeriss-cs5} focused on how individual
architectural parameters, such as register sizes and PE aspect
ratios, interact with the fusion mapping.
This section shifts perspective and asks the central question of
the thesis: \emph{does layer fusion actually improve the end-to-end
efficiency of a DNN accelerator, and under what conditions?}

To answer this, we define three execution modes on the same
architecture instance, which is sized to support full fusion of the
entire workload (Scenario~A):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Full fusion.}
        All convolutional layers are fused into a single mapping.
        Intermediate activations are kept entirely on chip, reducing
        DRAM traffic to the network's input and final output only.
  \item \textbf{Partial fusion (sum).}
        Consecutive layer pairs are fused independently.
        The total cost is the sum of all pair-wise fused evaluations.
        DRAM traffic is reduced for each pair, but intermediate
        activations between non-adjacent layers still traverse DRAM.
  \item \textbf{Single-layer (sum).}
        Each layer is mapped in isolation, and the total cost is
        the sum of all individual layer evaluations.
        Every intermediate activation is written to and read back
        from DRAM.
\end{enumerate}

The architecture used for each workload is the same full-fusion
configuration determined by the PE and memory sizing studies
(Table~\ref{tab:fusion-auto-configs}).
Running partial and single-layer modes on a full-fusion-sized
architecture provides a fair comparison because all three modes
share identical hardware; only the mapping strategy differs.

The following analysis is organised around three metrics, each
normalised to the full-fusion value, and concludes with the
latency savings summary.

\subsubsection{Results}

% ── Energy ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\paragraph{Energy (Figure~\ref{fig:fusion-energy-norm}).}
Full fusion is \emph{always} the most energy-expensive mode,
for every workload and on both architectures.
Single-layer execution consumes between $4.5\%$ (VGG16, DepFiN)
and $78.3\%$ (FSRCNN, DepFiN) of the full-fusion energy, while
partial fusion sits closer to the full-fusion level, ranging from
$66.8\%$ (VGG16, Eyeriss) to $109.6\%$ (FSRCNN, DepFiN).

The energy penalty of full fusion arises from the on-chip memory
overhead.
Fusing all layers requires substantially larger local storage,
such as DepFiN's weight memory growing from 19\,KB (single) to
14\,366\,KB (full) for VGG16, or Eyeriss's WReg rising from
a few hundred to 1\,200~words per PE.
Although fusion eliminates most DRAM traffic, every on-chip data
access passes through these enlarged memories, and the per-access
energy scales with memory size.
The energy trade-off is therefore governed by the ratio of
on-chip-access energy increase to DRAM-access energy saved.
In all eight architecture--workload combinations tested, the
enlarged on-chip memories outweigh the DRAM savings, making full
fusion the most energy-intensive mode.

On Eyeriss, the energy penalty is especially severe: the
full-to-single energy ratio reaches $36.9\times$ for VGG16 and
$25.3\times$ for ResNet18, compared to $22.3\times$ and
$11.4\times$ on DepFiN.
This disparity reflects the Eyeriss register architecture, where
each PE replicates the enlarged WReg locally.
With 16\,384~PEs for VGG16, the aggregate register capacity is
$1{,}200 \times 16{,}384 = 19.7$~million words, all of which
contribute to the dynamic energy whenever the weight register is
accessed.
DepFiN avoids this replication by housing weights in a shared
off-PE weight memory.

The activation-dominant workloads (FSRCNN, MC-CNN) exhibit
smaller energy penalties than the weight-dominant ones (VGG16,
ResNet18).
On DepFiN, the FSRCNN full-to-single ratio is only $1.3\times$
because the fused feature memory (576\,KB) is modest and the
weight memory remains tiny (19\,KB).
MC-CNN reaches $2.8\times$ on DepFiN, while on Eyeriss both
FSRCNN and MC-CNN sit at $6.4\times$ and $10.5\times$
respectively, reflecting the register replication overhead.

% ── Latency ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
\paragraph{Latency (Figure~\ref{fig:fusion-latency-norm}).}
Unlike energy, full fusion does deliver latency benefits, but
their magnitude depends on the workload and architecture.

For the activation-dominant workloads, full fusion is clearly
faster on both architectures.
FSRCNN enjoys a $47.6\%$ latency saving on DepFiN and $45.6\%$
on Eyeriss, while MC-CNN saves $42.3\%$ on DepFiN and $15.7\%$
on Eyeriss (Figure~\ref{fig:fusion-latency-savings}).
These workloads have large intermediate-activation volumes
relative to their weight volumes, so eliminating the DRAM
round-trips for activations removes the dominant bottleneck from
the execution timeline.

For the weight-dominant workloads the picture is more nuanced.
On Eyeriss, full fusion still reduces latency compared to the
single-layer sum: VGG16 saves $21.2\%$ and ResNet18 saves $7.8\%$.
On DepFiN, however, full fusion is \emph{slower} than the
single-layer sum: VGG16 loses $21.1\%$ and ResNet18 loses
$30.3\%$.
The key difference is that DepFiN's full-fusion configuration
must allocate a very large weight memory (10--14\,MB), and the
mapper requires more temporal passes to fill it.
The additional scheduling overhead exceeds the latency saved from
reduced DRAM traffic, producing a net latency increase.
Eyeriss avoids this penalty because its distributed register
architecture can parallelize weight loading across PEs.

Partial fusion tracks the full-fusion latency closely in most
cases: the ratio ranges from $0.87\times$ (ResNet18, DepFiN) to
$1.03\times$ (ResNet18, Eyeriss), indicating that the latency
benefit of fusion is captured almost entirely by pairwise fusion,
with little additional gain from fusing all layers
simultaneously.

% ── EDP ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\paragraph{Energy-Delay Product
  (Figure~\ref{fig:fusion-edp-norm}).}
The EDP combines energy and latency into a single efficiency
metric and delivers the most nuanced verdict on fusion.

On DepFiN, full fusion wins on EDP for only one workload:
FSRCNN, where the large latency saving ($47.6\%$) outweighs the
modest energy penalty ($1.3\times$), yielding an EDP that is
$33\%$ lower than the single-layer sum
(normalised single\,=\,$1.494$).
For all other DepFiN workloads, single-layer execution dominates:
MC-CNN's single-layer EDP is $0.621\times$ the full-fusion value,
ResNet18 is $0.067\times$, and VGG16 is $0.040\times$.

On Eyeriss, the results are starker: the single-layer sum is
the EDP winner for every workload.
Even FSRCNN, whose $45.6\%$ latency saving is comparable to
DepFiN, has a single-layer EDP of $0.288\times$ full fusion
because the Eyeriss energy penalty is $6.4\times$ rather than
$1.3\times$.
The pattern holds consistently: MC-CNN single
$= 0.113\times$, VGG16 $= 0.034\times$, ResNet18
$= 0.043\times$.

Partial fusion performs slightly better than full fusion on EDP
for the weight-dominant workloads, with normalised values of
$0.659$ (VGG16, DepFiN) and $0.716$ (VGG16, Eyeriss), because
the smaller per-pair memory requirement reduces the energy
penalty.
For the activation-dominant workloads, partial fusion is within
$1$--$13\%$ of full fusion, offering no meaningful advantage.

% ── DRAM Traffic ────────────────────────────────────────────
\paragraph{DRAM traffic reduction.}
The fundamental premise of layer fusion is the elimination of
intermediate DRAM transfers, and the data confirm that this
mechanism works as expected.
Full fusion reduces total DRAM traffic (reads + writes) by
$94.8\%$ for FSRCNN, $85.3\%$ for MC-CNN, $59.9\%$ for VGG16,
and $25.1\%$ for ResNet18, relative to single-layer execution.
These numbers are identical across the two architectures because
the DRAM access pattern depends on the dataflow mapping, not on
the on-chip memory technology.

The reduction is largest for the activation-dominant workloads
because their intermediate activations comprise the overwhelming
majority of total DRAM traffic in single-layer mode.
For the weight-dominant workloads, weights dominate the transfer
volume, and since weights are loaded from DRAM regardless of
fusion, the relative saving is smaller.

% ── Key Takeaway ────────────────────────────────────────────
\paragraph{Key takeaway.}
Full fusion achieves its primary goal, the elimination of
inter-layer DRAM traffic, delivering reductions of
$25$--$95\%$ depending on the workload.
This translates into significant latency improvements for
activation-dominant workloads ($42$--$48\%$ on DepFiN,
$16$--$46\%$ on Eyeriss).
However, the on-chip memory overhead required to support full
fusion imposes a substantial energy penalty, ranging from
$1.3\times$ (FSRCNN, DepFiN) to $36.9\times$ (VGG16, Eyeriss),
which dominates the EDP trade-off.
As a result, full fusion wins on EDP in only one of the eight
architecture--workload combinations tested (FSRCNN on DepFiN);
in all other cases, single-layer execution is more
energy-efficient overall.
The implication is that the benefit of fusion is primarily a
latency benefit: it accelerates execution at the cost of
increased total energy.
Whether this trade-off is acceptable depends on whether the
design priority is throughput (latency-sensitive) or energy
efficiency (battery or thermal budget).
